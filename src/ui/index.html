What's included. Each bracket rate is applied to the portion of taxable income in that bracket, federal plus provincial. With basic personal amounts on, each government's amount is credited at its lowest bracket rate (federal: 14% of $16,452), and a credit can reduce tax to zero but not below. The blended rate is total tax ÷ actual income received; the marginal rate is the tax on the next dollar, including credit phase-outs.

Deductions. Deductions such as RRSP contributions are subtracted from total income to get taxable income. The Max RRSP link uses 18% of the employment income entered, up to the 2026 dollar limit of $33,810; your real limit is based on last year's earned income and any unused room, as shown on your notice of assessment.

Phase-outs. The federal amount (and Yukon's, which mirrors it) drops from $16,452 to $14,829 between $181,440 and $258,482. Manitoba's $15,780 phases out to zero between $200,000 and $400,000. Phase-outs are based on net income.

Income tax details. Each person gets the Canada employment amount ($1,501, also in Yukon) and credits for base CPP/QPP contributions and EI/QPIP premiums, federally and in every province except Quebec, which instead allows a deduction for workers (6% of employment income, up to $1,450). Enhanced CPP/QPP contributions are deducted from income. Ontario adds its surtax (20% of Ontario tax over $5,818 and 36% over $7,446), the Ontario tax reduction ($300 plus $555 per child; the child amount is 2025's $544 indexed by 2%) and the LIFT credit (up to $875). BC applies its tax reduction (up to $690, phased out between $25,570 and $44,952 of net income). Low-income tax reductions are applied in Newfoundland and Labrador, New Brunswick, PEI and Nova Scotia (New Brunswick's and Newfoundland and Labrador's family threshold are 2025 figures indexed by 2%). Saskatchewan's child amount ($8,358 per child) and the Nova Scotia and Nunavut credits for children under 6 ($1,200 per child) are claimed by the parent who claims the children. A single parent claims the eligible dependant amount; in Quebec a single adult gets the amount for a person living alone. Manitoba's brackets are frozen at $47,000 and $100,000; CRA's 2026 rate page shows indexed thresholds, but Manitoba's 2025 and 2026 budgets keep them unindexed.

Payroll. CPP: 4.95% base plus 1% enhanced on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, and 4% on earnings up to $85,000. Quebec's QPP is 5.30% base plus the same enhanced rates. EI: 1.63% of earnings up to $68,900, or 1.30% in Quebec, where QPIP adds 0.43% up to $103,000. Ontario adds its Health Premium (up to $900, based on taxable income), and the Northwest Territories and Nunavut withhold a 2% payroll tax. Employment and other income is treated as employment income.

Child benefits. July 2026 to June 2027 amounts, applied to current-year income: the Canada Child Benefit ($8,157 per child under 6 or $6,883 aged 6–17, reduced above $38,237 of family net income), the Ontario Child Benefit (up to $1,760 per child), Quebec's family allowance ($3,068 to $1,221 per child, plus the $127 school supplies supplement, applied to children aged 6–17), the B.C. family benefit, the Alberta Child and Family Benefit, the Nova Scotia Child Benefit, the PEI Child Benefit, New Brunswick's Child Tax Benefit and Working Income Supplement, and the Yukon Child Benefit, all from CRA's T4114 booklet for July 2026 to June 2027 or the province's own rules. For the NWT, Newfoundland and Labrador and Nunavut benefits the amounts and the income where the reduction starts are official, but the reduction rate isn't published: the NWT and Newfoundland and Labrador benefits are tapered in a straight line to the published end points ($80,000 and $38,193), and Nunavut's uses the 3% / 5% rates in its 2026 Bill 4. These approximations only affect families below about $80,000. Saskatchewan has no child benefit; Manitoba's (for incomes under about $15,000), Newfoundland and Labrador's prenatal–early childhood nutrition supplement and Nunavut's territorial workers' supplement aren't modelled, and neither are childcare costs or credits.

Refundable credits. Counted when "Include in totals" is on, for July 2026 to June 2027: the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (the renamed GST/HST credit: $445 per adult, $234 per child and a single supplement of up to $234, less 5% of family net income over $46,432); the Canada Workers Benefit basic amount for 2026 ($1,665 single or $2,869 family, 27% of working income over $3,000, less 15% of net income over $27,392 or $31,251; Alberta, Nunavut and Quebec use their own versions, and these 2026 values are CRA's 2025 figures indexed by 2% until CRA publishes them); the Ontario sales tax credit and Ontario energy and property tax credit; Quebec's solidarity tax credit and work premium; B.C.'s renter's tax credit; Manitoba's renters and homeowners affordability credits; the Saskatchewan low-income tax credit; the New Brunswick HST credit; the Nova Scotia affordable living tax credit; the PEI essentials benefit; and the Newfoundland and Labrador income supplement. Estimates: Saskatchewan's reduction rate (3.03%) is worked out from CRA's published start and end points, Manitoba's renters credit is taken at its $625 maximum, and Quebec's solidarity credit gives the living-alone amount to any household with one adult. Not modelled: the Workers Benefit disability supplement, senior-specific credits, the Northern Ontario energy credit and Quebec's northern-village supplement.

Property tax. Home value × the typical residential rate in each province's largest city for 2026: Vancouver 0.30%, Calgary 0.66%, Saskatoon 1.34%, Winnipeg 1.38%, Toronto 0.76%, Montreal 0.90% (school tax may be extra), Moncton 1.36%, Halifax 0.61%, Charlottetown 1.67% and St. John's 0.91%. Whitehorse, Yellowknife and Iqaluit use a rough 1.0% estimate. Land transfer tax is a one-time cost and isn't included.

What's not included. Age, pension, disability, medical, donation and other personal credits; pension income splitting; fuel, excise and corporate taxes. Newfoundland and Labrador's amount reflects its April 2026 budget increase to $13,094, effective January 1, 2026.

Investment income. For investments held outside a TFSA or RRSP. Enter capital gains net of the year's capital losses; half of a net gain is added to taxable income. A net capital loss adds nothing to taxable income and can't reduce tax on salary or dividends, but it does lower the actual income the blended rate is measured against. The loss can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely against future capital gains. Eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and non-eligible dividends by 15%, then federal and provincial dividend tax credits are applied at each government's 2026 rate. The blended rate divides total tax by actual cash income (salary plus the full gain and dividends received). Foreign dividends get no gross-up or dividend tax credit, so they're taxed like salary. Tax withheld abroad is counted in the total and credited back as a foreign tax credit, federally first and then provincially, up to the Canadian tax on that income; any withholding above that limit stays as extra tax. Grossed-up dividends count as income for the personal amount phase-outs. Not modelled: deducting foreign withholding above 15%, the alternative minimum tax (which can apply with large gains or dividends),. Income inside a TFSA is not taxed; RRSP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

Defaults. Each person starts at $75,000 of employment income. Household spending starts at the one-person estimate and moves to the two-person estimate when you add a partner.

Spending by household size. The starting spending amount is a rough estimate for the number of adults plus children, rounded to the nearest $100: $2,000 for one person, $2,800 for two, $3,500 for three, $4,000 for four, $4,500 for five and $4,900 for six. It starts from average Ontario household spending of $81,975 in 2023 (about 2.5 people), minus shelter ($29,312), groceries (about 70% of food spending), health care, and other mostly exempt costs such as insurance, childcare and tuition. That leaves about $38,000 a year, or $3,200 a month. This is scaled to each household size by the square root of the number of people, the same adjustment Statistics Canada uses to compare incomes across household sizes, because a second person adds less than a first. For context, Statistics Canada's 2024 Canadian Income Survey puts median after-tax income at $75,500 per family or single person, and its 2023 Survey of Household Spending puts average Ontario household spending at $81,975, of which $29,312 is shelter. The Fraser Institute estimates the average Canadian family paid $6,972 in sales taxes in 2025, a figure that also counts sales tax built into business costs.

Sales tax. Annual sales tax is monthly taxable spending, adjusted by the frugality setting, × 12 × the province's combined rate. PST, QST and Manitoba's RST are charged on the price before GST, so the rates simply add. The rate applies to the whole amount entered; provinces with their own PST exempt some extra items, and Ontario still charges 8% RST on some insurance, neither of which is modelled. Sales tax is shown as a share of household income alongside income tax.

Household. Canada has no joint filing, so each person is taxed on their own income and the household figures add the two together. If the second person is a spouse or common-law partner and basic personal amounts are on, the higher-income partner claims the spouse amount for the other: each government's spouse amount is reduced by the lower earner's net income (above a small threshold in some provinces) and credited at the lowest bracket rate. Quebec has no provincial spouse amount; instead the lower earner's unused basic credit is transferred to their partner. Marginal rates are household rates: the change in the household's total tax when the selected person earns one more dollar.

Quebec. CRA's page refers Quebec to Revenu Québec, so its brackets come from Revenu Québec's 2026 rates. Quebec residents' federal tax is reduced by the 16.5% Quebec abatement, applied here.
